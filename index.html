How Silicon Valley's tech boom since 2010 drove a chain reaction of housing displacement across California.

Topic Summary

A wave of displacement swept across the state

As the tech industry boomed in the 2010s, especially in the Bay Area, it brought something more than just wealth. As well-paid tech workers moved into California cities, rents went up and longtime residents were forced out. Some communities even vanished.

This site traces that chain reaction in four cities: San Francisco, Oakland, San Jose and Sacramento. This website will explore how tech-driven displacement can act like a modern form of colonization by separating people from their homes and resources.

01
San Francisco · Bay Area

San Francisco

Ground Zero

San Francisco was the first city to fully face the effects of tech driven displacement. As Silicon Valley companies expanded into the city, many high paying workers moved in. This phenomenon pressured the housing market that already had limited supply and few protection for tenants.

How San Francisco Changed: 1990 vs 2010
1990 — Majority middle-income (white)
2010 — Majority upper-income (green)
Conway, Nick. NickConwayBlog.wordpress.com, 2016.

These maps illustrate how San Francisco has changed over time from 1990 to 2010. In 1990, most neighborhoods were marked in white which indicates they were middle income areas where 60% of the residents lived. By 2010, the map turned mostly green which represents upper income neighborhoods and the middle income share fell to just 41% (Conway, 2016). The Mission District, located in the center of the map, also shifted from lower income in 1990 to middle income by 2010. This change did not happen because residents became wealthier, but because many of them had to leave.

Between 2010 and 2020, thousands of Latino families faced eviction due to rising rents and Ellis Act evictions (Opillard, 2015). As tech workers moved in, landlords exploited the high demand. In response, the community organized a Google Bus protest in 2013 where they blocked private tech shuttle buses that symbolized the tech industry's influence in working class neighborhoods.

Displacement also cut off communities from parks and green spaces that were vital to the neighborhood's life. As wealthier residents moved in, there was increased investment in the environment. This left displaced communities in areas that have worse air quality, less green space and higher environmental risk.

02
Oakland · East Bay

Oakland

The Spillover

As San Francisco got too expensive for people to live in, many people moved to Oakland. However, the problem followed them as well. In West Oakland, long-established Black communities were being displaced because rent prices skyrocketed due to tech workers moving in.

Oakland Unlawful Detainers & Rental Prices, 2005–2016
Anti-Eviction Mapping Project · antievictionmap.com · 2016

From 2005 to 2015 Oakland experienced 32,402 eviction notices and 20,340 foreclosures with the heaviest impact on West and East Oakland, areas that have long been home to Oakland's Black communities (Anti-Eviction Mapping Project, 2016). Around 2011, average monthly apartment rent was about $1,600, but by 2016 it had gone up to $2,912 which is over an 80% increase in a 5 year span. The areas shown as most problematic on the map are the neighborhoods with the deepest and longest history within Oakland's Black community.

During the 2010s, West Oakland, which had been a strong and lively center for Black culture, lost a huge portion of their community. In fact, by 2010 Oakland had more white residents than Black ones for the first time since the 1970s and West Oakland's access to BART made it even more appealing for tech workers commuting to San Francisco (Harnett, KQED, 2014). The Black neighborhood, who are already affected by decades of redlining and highway construction, have to face a wave of gentrification because of increasing rent. Families who had been there for generations were forced to move out of their homes.

The San Francisco Federal Reserve's figures show Oakland's white population increased sharply in the 2010s. At the same time, the Black population decreased by more than a third. Low income residents who were displaced were more likely to leave Oakland and even the entire Bay Area as it became harder and harder to find somewhere to live (Mujahid et al., SF Fed, 2021).

03
San Jose · Silicon Valley

San Jose

Silicon Valley Core

San Jose is right in the middle of Silicon Valley, with Apple, Google and other big tech companies all around it. Even though it has been the city most directly impacted by the growth of the tech industry, it has the weakest tenant protection in the Bay Area. This has made people with low incomes to be forced to move out.

San Jose, CA

In East San Jose, Latino neighborhoods have faced a lot of pressure as tech workers search for places to live close to where they work. According to city figures, 24% of Latino renters in San Jose are spending a dangerously large amount of their income on housing. The neighborhoods at highest risk of losing residents are concentrated in East San Jose (San Jose Spotlight, 2024). San Jose did not have strong rules about how much landlords could increase rent or when they could sell. As a result, it accelerates the process of people being pushed from neighborhoods that have been home to immigrant families for many years.

A study from UC Berkeley's Urban Displacement Project in Santa Clara County discovered that almost half of the population surveyed thought that they would likely have to move out in the next year. And over half of those who had been displaced stated that their landlord had taken retaliatory actions which contributed to them being pushed out with Spanish speaking tenants especially singled out (Urban Displacement Project, 2019).

San Jose became the clearest example of how the growth of the tech industry leads to people being displaced. The city is surrounded by the campuses that produced the wealth which are now forcing people to leave. This makes the link between the industry and displacement hard to overlook.

04
Sacramento · Central Valley

Sacramento

The Last Refuge

Sacramento used to be where people could stay when they could not afford the Bay Area. However, that changed after 2020 when remote work allowed tech employees to move to Sacramento and still work in a high-paying job which led to higher rents and community displacement.

Sacramento, CA

From 2019 to the beginning of 2022, average rent in Sacramento went from $1,390 to $1,856 which is a 33% increase in three years. This happens due to a lot of people from the Bay Area now wanting to live there. In 2022, Sacramento's homeless count increased by 67%, a bigger jump than anywhere else in California (Sacramento News & Review, 2022).

Sacramento is now the last stop in a long line of people being forced to move, and what happened there shows that simply going to a cheaper place will not solve the problem when things that are causing people to be displaced are much larger than it seems. The new 'displacement' spread until it reached everywhere in California.

Works Cited

Opillard, Florian. "Resisting the Politics of Displacement in the San Francisco Bay Area: Anti-Gentrification Activism in the Tech Boom 2.0." European Journal of American Studies, vol. 10, no. 3, 2015, doi.org/10.4000/ejas.11322.

Florian Opillard is a geographer, researcher at IRSEM in the "Defense and Society" team and associate researcher at the Center for Research and Documentation on the Americas (IHEAL-CREDA). This peer-reviewed article is based on ethnographic fieldwork in San Francisco's tenant movement. It looks at the context of gentrification and community resistance during 2014. Opillard shows how a surge in tech industry money led to a rise in evictions and tenant harassment. The article also explores how activist groups organized in response. It links current patterns of displacement to the longer history of settler colonialism in California.

Unlike quantitative studies, this source focuses on the human experience of displacement instead of just numbers. It is peer-reviewed and based on firsthand fieldwork, which makes it a credible and detailed account of how the tech boom changed San Francisco neighborhoods.

This source directly relates to my project because it supports my main argument that tech-driven displacement is a modern form of colonization. It will assist me in writing the San Francisco section of my website, where I analyze how the tech boom displaced the Latino community in the Mission District. The article also helps me connect my argument to course themes of power, control, and resistance, which my GSI highlighted as important for this project.

Mujahid, Mahasin S., et al. "Gentrification and Displacement in the San Francisco Bay Area: A Comparison of Measurement Approaches." International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, vol. 16, no. 13, 2019, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6616964/.

Mujahid is a professor of epidemiology at UC Berkeley, and her co-authors include researchers from UCSF and other Bay Area institutions. This peer-reviewed article is written by public health researchers who explore the health effects of gentrification in nine counties of the San Francisco Bay Area. The authors compare three methods for measuring gentrification across 1,580 census tracts from 2000 to 2013. They find that nearly 20% of Bay Area neighborhoods went through significant gentrification or displacement, especially in the Mission District, SoMa, and West Oakland. The study claims that displacement is not solely a housing issue; it is a public health crisis that disrupts communities' access to environmental resources, social networks, and stable living conditions.

This is a credible source supported by thorough census-level data and published in a recognized public health journal. Unlike the ethnographic approach taken by Opillard, this study offers quantitative, data-based evidence that enhances the personal narratives on my website. Its emphasis on environmental resources also reinforces the environmental justice aspect of my project.

This source is particularly useful because it bridges housing displacement and environmental access, which my GSI specifically asked me to address. I will use its data on gentrification patterns across Oakland and San Francisco to support the city sections of my website, and its public health framing to argue that displacement is an environmental justice issue, not just an economic one.
